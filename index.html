The money factor is the lease’s interest rate — disguised as a tiny decimal

Every lease payment is depreciation plus a finance charge. The money factor sets that finance charge. Move the sliders to watch the same deal get cheaper or more expensive when the factor, residual, or cap cost changes.

Definition

What is a money factor?

A lease payment has two main pieces: the depreciation charge (how much the car is expected to lose during the term) and the finance charge (the cost of borrowing). The money factor is the lease world’s version of an interest rate. It is written as a small decimal such as 0.00125 instead of a percentage.

Those decimals look harmless. They are not. Multiply the money factor by 2,400 to get an approximate APR:

0.000501.2% APR
0.001002.4% APR
0.001253.0% APR
0.001503.6% APR
0.002004.8% APR
0.002506.0% APR

A dealer quoting 0.00200 sounds smaller than saying “you are paying 4.8% on this lease.” That format is part of why most shoppers never question the rate.

The math

How the money factor hits the monthly payment

Unlike a loan, the finance charge is applied to both the amount you are using up (cap cost) and the part you are not buying (residual). That is why the factor is multiplied by the sum of the two.

Finance charge / mo = (Cap cost + Residual) × Money factor
Depreciation / mo = (Cap cost − Residual) ÷ Term
Base payment = Depreciation + Finance charge

The Vantage article’s worked example uses a $40,000 cap cost and a $24,000 residual:

  • Money factor 0.00100 (2.4% APR) → finance charge $64 / month
  • Money factor 0.00200 (4.8% APR) → finance charge $128 / month
  • Money factor 0.00250 (6.0% APR) → finance charge $160 / month

Best vs. worst in that set is $96 per month, or $3,456 over 36 months — determined by a number most buyers never see.

Why multiply by 2,400?

Interest is charged on the average balance of the lease, which sits halfway between cap cost and residual. A yearly rate divided into months is APR ÷ 12 ÷ 100. Splitting the balance in half folds another ÷ 2 into the same shortcut, which is APR ÷ 2,400. So:

The part dealers skip

The markup nobody volunteers

Manufacturers set a base (or “buy”) money factor each month by vehicle, term, mileage, and credit tier — Toyota Financial, BMW Financial, Ally, Chase Auto, and the rest publish these to dealers. Dealers may mark that number up and keep the spread.

If the base factor is 0.00100 and the quoted factor is 0.00150, the 0.00050 markup on a $64,000 combined cap + residual is $32 / month, or $1,152 over 36 months. A typical 0.00050 markup can add $20–$50 a month, which is $720–$1,800 over three years. It is legal. It is common. It does not appear unless you ask.

Credit tier matters too. Tier 1 (often 720+) gets the lowest base factor; lower tiers pay more. Two people leasing the same car at the same store can be on different factors.

What to do

How to protect yourself

  • Ask: “What money factor are you using?” Silence is a red flag.
  • Ask: “Is that the manufacturer’s base factor, or has it been marked up?”
  • Look up current base factors in lease forums before you sit down. Walking in with the buy rate is leverage.
  • Get a credit-union loan pre-approval. If they offer 4% and the lease converts to 6%, the lease rate is inflated.
  • A lower factor is always better all else equal — but residual value can offset a cheap rate. Optimize both, not just one.

Vantage, the source of this explainer, is a New Jersey auto broker that says it locks manufacturer base factors with no dealer markup. This page is an educational model of their article, not an offer from them.

FAQ

Common questions

What is a money factor on a car lease?

The lease equivalent of an interest rate, written as a small decimal such as 0.00125. Multiply by 2,400 for an approximate APR. It sets the finance-charge slice of the monthly payment.

How do I convert money factor to APR?

Money factor × 2,400. Example: 0.00150 × 2,400 = 3.6% APR. Close enough to compare against a loan quote, even if it is not a legally precise APR disclosure.

Can a dealer mark up the money factor?

Yes. The manufacturer sets a base factor. Dealers may add a markup and keep it. They are not required to disclose the markup unless you ask.

Does credit score change the factor?

Yes. Each finance arm publishes different factors by credit tier, and those grids change monthly. Better credit usually means a lower base factor.

Is a lower money factor always better?

All else equal, yes. In the real world a subsidized factor sometimes comes with a weaker residual, or the reverse. The payment is the combination of both.

What if the car is worth more than the residual at turn-in?

You have equity. You can buy at the residual and keep or sell the car. That is one reason a high residual is valuable even before the monthly math.
